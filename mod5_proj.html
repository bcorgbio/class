Module 5 Project

Introduction

Sharks are an ancient group of cartilaginous fishes in the class Chondrichthyes, subclass Elasmobranchii, and subdivision Selachimorpha. The selachians include over 500 species that are further placed in two superorders: Squalomorphii and Galeomorphii. Modern sharks originated nearly 200 million years ago (Grogan, Lund, and Greenfest-Allen (2012)) and, since then, have diversified into nearly every major marine ecosystem. This evolutionary success has been attributed to the diversity of their locomotor modes and associated body shape (Lauder and Di Santo (2015),Thomson and Simanek (1977)). A modest body of work devoted to the ecomorphology of sharks has focused largely on caudal fin shape as it relates to ecology and locomotor modes Wilga and Lauder (2004). Sharks have heterocercal tails in which the caudal part of the notochord flexes dorsally and supports a large dorsal lobe of the fin, while, with more than a few exceptions, the ventral lobe is small.





(A) The phylogenetic relationships and systematic placement of major groups of sharks and (B) Thomson and Simanek (1977)’s ecomorphological groups. Figure from Sternes and Shimada (2020).



In their seminal work on shark ecomorphology, Thomson and Simanek (1977) put forth that there were four shark morphotypes:

  1. A group characterized by having a deep body, large pectoral fins, a symmetrical caudal fin with a narrow peduncle (i.e., base to the fin) and high-aspect ratio. A white shark represents this group.
  2. A group characterized by a body less deep than Group 1 and a caudal fin that is swept (i.e., large heterocercal angle–the angle between the upper and lower lobes of the fin). This group includes most carcharhinid sharks such as the bull shark.
  3. A group with a large head, blunt snout, a body that has anteriorly placed pectoral fins and posteriorly placed dorsal and pelvic fins, and a caudal fin with a low heterocercal angle and small ventral lobe. Catsharks (family Scyliorhinidae) and dogfish (order Squaliformes) represent this group.
  4. A group characterized by a caudal fin with a higher aspect angle similar to that of Group 2 but lacking an anal fin, e.g., the dalatiids.




A white shark, Carcharodon carcharias, typical of Group 1.



A bull shark, Carcharhinus leucas, typical of Group 2.



A dogfish of the genus Squalus typical of Group 3.


A kitefin shark, Dalatias licha, typical of Group 4.



Sternes and Shimada (2020) used geometric morphometrics and a multivariate analysis to evaluate the groups proposed by Thomson and Simanek (1977) and found that sharks fell into one of two morphotypes (A and B). Their Group A was characterized by an elongate body, posteriorly placed dorsal fin, and a swept caudal fin, while Group B was characterized by a deeper body, anteriorly placed dorsal fins, and a less swept caudal fin. Thomson and Simanek (1977) suggested that the shape of species in these two broad ecomorphs evolved due to differences in ecological niche and locomotor behavior. Specifically, they posited that the species of elongate Group A are typically benthic and swim like eels whereas the deeper bodied species of Group B are often pelagic and swim like salmon or tunas.



From Sternes and Shimada (2020) who found sharks could be assigned to two morphotype groups: A, elongate body, posteriorly placed dorsal fin, and a swept caudal fin; B, deeper body, anteriorly placed dorsal fins, and a less swept caudal fin.



Although the work of Thomson and Simanek (1977) and later Sternes and Shimada (2020) points to interesting patterns in shark body shape, neither study explicitly studied the role of habitat—that is, pelagic or benthic—in influencing the evolution of body shape. In particular, Sternes and Shimada (2020) merely observed that their two groups with different body shapes often varied in their habitat preference. In addition, Sternes and Shimada (2020) made no explicit, quantitative phylogenetic test of their patterns. This leaves open an important question: Do pelagic and benthic sharks have significantly different body shapes? Subsequently, we might also ask, Has the morphology of pelagic and benthic sharks evolved at the same rate? These are the questions we will take on in this project.

These questions represent an important thrust of evolutionary research: what macroevolutionary events have contributed to diversity, whether in species or phenotype. To answer questions like these requires that we essentially recapitulate the Sternes and Shimada (2020) body-shape data set using landmark-based geometric morphometrics. This is a relatively straightforward, if tedious, method of capturing shape variation in a group of interest. For this, you’ll digitize 14 homologous landmarks on images of some 265 species of sharks. Using the best practices in the field of geometric morphometrics to align our landmarks and extract shape information, we’ll assess morphological disparity in a phylogenetic framework and assess the difference in rates of morphological evolution between pelagic and benthic sharks.

We first began with images of drawings from Compagno (1984), Compagno (2001), and Ebert, Dando, and Fowler (2021), all authoritative texts on the identification, ecology, and evolution of sharks. Now, if we relied on only these drawings, we might expect to take some heat from reasonable people who contend that drawings are abstractions of specimens rather than direct representations, like a photograph would be. So, we developed a custom R workflow to obtain images for many of our 265 species from three public biodiversity data sources: iNaturalist, [FishBase], and the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF). iNaturalist images were retrieved using the rinat package, querying observations by scientific name and downloading photographs at the largest available resolution. FishBase images were scraped directly from species photo pages (https://www.fishbase.us/photos ) using HTML parsing, and GBIF images were accessed via the rgbif API, restricting searches to still images associated with preserved specimens. This resulted in a cache of images for the ~265 species included in a recent phylogeny of the elasmobranchs (Stein et al. (2018)).

Because we’ll be comparing body shape in sharks, this project is by definition a comparative one and we’ll need a phylogeny for the group. Fortunately, a relatively recent phylogenomic analysis of over 500 species of elasmobranchs (sharks + batoids) has been published by Stein et al. (2018). Unfortunately, there is some phylogenetic discordance in this study. Specifically, when researchers analyze phylogenetic relationships, they typically repeat their analysis in a process known as bootstrapping. In phylogenetics, bootstrapping is conducted using the columns of the character matrix. Each pseudoreplicate contains the same number of species (rows) and characters (columns) randomly sampled from the original matrix, with replacement. A phylogeny is reconstructed from each pseudoreplicate, with the same methods used to reconstruct the phylogeny from the original data. This often results in a dataset of trees that have conflicting relationships—that is, phylogenetic uncertainty—and this was the case for the Stein et al. (2018) study. We’ll have to account for this in our analysis. But, more on that later . . .

In any case, we’ve used the taxa studied in this paper to find our images and we’ll use a tree file representing the phylogeny for our comparative analysis.

Geometric morphometrics

As you have learned in class and in your reading, researchers interested in comparing shape change between species often use landmark-based geometric morphometrics. A geometric morphometric study such as ours generally follows these steps:

  1. Data collection: select landmarks of interest, usually through digitization of specimen images.
  2. Data standardization: Make landmarks comparable across all specimens, usually through superimposition.
  3. Analysis: choose a statistical approach appropriate to the questions.
  4. Interpretation: use the outcome of the statistical analysis to assess the original questions.

Superimposition

After digitizing landmarks, we must remove the distracting variations of size, orientation, and position that can vary between specimens and thus add noise to the analysis of shape. This can be mitigated by using a superimposition method. Generalized Procrustes analysis (GPA) is the superimposition method of choice nowadays. GPA removes the variation of size, orientation, and position by superimposing the landmarks in a common coordinate system. The landmarks for all specimens are optimally translated, rotated, and scaled based on a least-squares estimation. The first step is translation and rotation to minimize the squared and summed differences (squared Procrustes distance) between landmarks on each specimen. Then the landmarks are individually scaled to the same units of centroid size. Centroid size is the square root of the sum of squared distances of the landmarks in configuration to their mean location. The translation, rotation, and scaling bring the landmark configurations for all specimens into a common coordinate system so that the only differing variables are based on shape alone. The new superimposed landmarks can now be analyzed in multivariate statistical analyses.

Analysis Part I: A Morphospace

In geometric morphometric analysis, researchers first turn to describing the axes of shape variation. Indeed, this is what Sternes and Shimada (2020) did to produce the figure above. This representation, which is what we’re after, is often termed a morphospace, a representation of the shapes the study group does and does not have.

To develop such a thing, biologists usually turn to principal components analysis (PCA) whereby a very complicated suite of variables is distilled into a set of fewer variables that describe the important variance among the original variables. The coordinates contained in our gpa object describe dozens of variables: scaled distances between all the landmarks. So we have many variables to consider and this becomes, very quickly, a serious multivariate problem. PCA is suited to multivariate analysis because it reduces the complexity of the data, taking many variables and distilling them into just a few. At its core, PCA produces a series of regressions, or vectors, that pass through the values of the original variables. This operation is performed iteratively, with the first vector (the first principal component, PC1), drawn through the variable space that accounts for the most variance in the data set. After the combination of variables forming PC1 is removed, a subsequent vector (PC2) is drawn through another unique variable space that describes the second greatest amount of variance. This continues until most of the variance is captured, resulting in scores of PCs. The first two or three, however, are usually enough to describe a healthy amount of variance.

To perform PCA, we’ll use geomorph’s gm.prcomp() function like so, working with the gpa object’s coordinates, that is, the 3-D array that contains the Procrustes aligned landmarks. Plotting reveals our first result.

#PCA
pca <- gm.prcomp(gpa$coords)

#visualize
plot(pca)

Wow, now we’re getting close to the Sternes and Shimada (2020) results and answering, at least in part, one of our questions: Does habitat result in different body shapes? But let’s break down what this plot means first. By default, when we plot a PCA object, the first two PCs are plotted, PC1 on the x axis, PC2 on the y. Each point represents the PC scores for a specimen in the data set. Notice that the amount of variance explained by our PCs is given and, as expected, PC1 contains most of the variance (55%) and PC2 considerably less (19%). If we plot again, but specify a different combination of PCs, say 1 and 3, we get a different plot, of course. Notice the variance explained by PC3 is predictably less than that of PC2.

plot(pca,1,3)

OK, so based on our PC plots, it looks as if we have two groupings, just like Sternes and Shimada (2020). But, do these groups correspond to largely pelagic vs. benthic species? It’s hard to tell. Analysis: Part II

Our PCA analysis reveals an interesting pattern. This, however, is not a quantitative assessment per se, merely a visual and qualitative result and not dissimilar to the qualitative results reported by Sternes and Shimada (2020). Several quantitative frameworks for assessing differences in shape exist. The geomorph package contains several, but we’ll use just one: a phylogenetically informed Procrustes ANOVA. The function procD.pgls() will do this for us. Like with our anole project in which we fit univariate traits with PGLS, procD.pgls() fits a phylogenetic generalized least-squares model for multivariate data under a Brownian motion model of evolution.

As with our PGLS model fitting in the anole project, we need a tree and the tree from Stein et al. (2018) mentioned above is the most comprehensive. To undertake the analysis with procD.pgls(), we must input the tree of course and a geomorph.dataframe, a special data frame that includes the coordinates and variables (i.e., habitat) that will be in the model. But, as we’ve discussed above, we have several specimens per species and thus multiple observations for each tip in the tree. procD.pgls() can’t handle that. A common solution to such a problem is to just calculate the mean values of each species. For this, we can turn to a custom function that uses geomorph’s mshape function to compute the mean shape of each species. It will compute the mean shape for each unique coordinate name, that is, species. Notice here that we reorder the resulting array of mean shapes along its third dimension according to the order of species (tip.label) in the tree, a standard practice with comparative methods in R.

#install abind package
#install.packages("abind")
library(abind)
#mean shape of uniquely named coordinates
mean_shape <- function(x){
  array(dim=3)
  sp <- dimnames(x$coords)[[3]]
  m_shapes <- list()
  for(s in unique(sp)){
    sp_n <- which(sp==s)
    
    m_shapes[[s]] <- mshape(x$coords[,,sp_n])
  }
  m_ <- abind(m_shapes,along=3)
  return(coord=m_)
}

#rename the coordinates
dimnames(gpa$coords)[[3]] <- PCA$sp

mean_coords <- mean_shape(gpa)[,,phy2[[1]]$tip.label]

Running anova(shark_pgls) produces a table that summarizes the relationship between habitat and body shape while accounting for phylogenetic relatedness. Our main interest is the habitat row, which tests whether benthic and pelagic sharks differ in mean shape. Several columns help us interpret this result.

Column

What does it tell us?

  • Df The degrees of freedom associated with each source of variation.

  • SS The sum of squares: the amount of variation attributed to habitat.

  • MS The mean square, calculated as SS / Df.

  • RsqThe proportion of variation attributed to habitat in the fitted phylogenetic model.

  • F The ratio of the habitat mean square to the residual mean square.

-Z How far the observed test statistic lies above or below its randomized expectation, measured in standard deviations.

Pr(>F) The permutation p-value: how often randomized results are at least as large as the observed F statistic.

To assess the magnitude of the habitat effect, look first at Rsq, or \(R^2\). For example, an R² of \(R^2\) would indicate that habitat accounts for 8% of shape variation in the fitted model, with the remaining 92% left in the residuals. This does not establish that habitat causes those differences, but it tells us how much variation is associated with habitat.

The Z column addresses a much different question: How unusual is the observed effect relative to what we would expect without a habitat effect? A Z score of 2 means that the observed log-F statistic is two standard deviations above the mean randomized value. A Z near zero indicates a result close to the randomized expectation. A negative Z indicates a smaller-than-expected statistic.

Finally, Pr(>F), the vaunted p value, provides the permutation test of statistical significance. A small p value indicates that an F statistic as large as ours is unlikele under the null hypothesis. However, statistical significance and effect magnitude are different properties. Habitat could explain a small proportion of shape variation and still yield a significant result. Therefore, its wise to interpret \(R^2\) alongside Z and p values

Because our analysis includes 500 alternative phylogenetic trees, we should also ask whether these conclusions depend on the tree used. More on that later. Nonetheless, based on analysis using one of the trees and for this limited data set of ~50 species, there’s no significant difference in mean shape between habitat groups.

Analysis Part III: Evolution in Parallel

Our secondary question, if you remember, was, “Has the body shape of pelagic and benthic sharks evolved at the same rate?”. This is a much different question than the first. We want to know, overall, if the shape of lineages that invaded a particular habitat has evolved faster or slower than those invading another habitat. For this, we’ll turn to the geomorph package once again, in particular, its compare.evol.rates() function. This function calculates net rates of shape evolution for groups of species on a phylogeny from a set of aligned landmarks. The approach is based on between-species differences in morphospace after phylogenetic transformation. In other words, after correcting for phylogenetic relatedness, it compares relative rates of shape change.

Because we’re correcting for phylogeny in this analysis, we need the tree from Stein et al. (2018) mentioned above. Once this tree file is downloaded, let’s read it in and store it in an object named phy. Notice you’ll need the phytools package and the function readNexus().

To undertake the analysis with compare.evol.rates(), we must specify A, the aligned coordinates in a landmark array of mean shape (mean_coords), a tree (more in just a bit), and our previously established vector of habitat groups describing to which group each species in the array belongs.

These are 500 trees produced by Stein et al. (2018) using the bootstrapping method mentioned earlier. Many of these trees are incongruent, that is, they represent different relationships for the sharks and this, in turn, represents some serious phylogenetic uncertainty.

Also notice that in our rate analysis and Procrustes PGLS, we used only one tree in this list (phy=phy2[[1]]). There are 499 other trees we could have used, perhaps each representing a different phylogenetic hypothesis. The results of a comparative analysis like this could, and likely will, vary according to the phylogenetic hypothesis used in it. So what to do?

This is a common problem faced by comparative biologists and, fortunately, there seems to be a consensus among us to repeat our comparative analysis using a random sample or, ambitiously, all of the trees that represent the phylogenetic uncertainty. By repeating an analysis, we produce a series of results based on new parameters (i.e., phylogenetic distance) to our model. In this case, the new parameters would come from using any or all of the trees. This establishes what is akin to a posterior sample and from this we can extract a posterior distribution of estimates, in our case, a distribution of evolutionary rates.

Therefore, we have before us the proposition of repeating our analysis, let’s say 10 times. To do this, we could randomly sample 10 of the trees and build a posterior distribution of evolutionary rates using a for loop.

Because our results are stored in a list, we can use lapply() to extract the rate comparisons. lapply() takes a list and applies a function to each element. In our case, we need to encode a custom function that extracts sigma.d.gp and sigma.d.ratio, the rate for each group and rate ratio between them, respectively. For this, we ask the function to work on every value of er_l, read it as x, then combine sigma.d.gp and sigma.d.ratio and store it in er_ temporarily. We then apply a name to the third value “ratio”, and return the vector. lapply() returns a list, so we combine all elements of it using do.call(). We apply the row-binding function rbind() to the list using the pipe and . and lastly, make the output a data frame.

er <- lapply(er_l,function(x) {
  er_ <- c(x$sigma.d.gp,x$sigma.d.ratio)
  names(er_)[3] <-"ratio"
  return(er_)
}) %>% do.call(rbind,.) %>% data.frame

We should, therefore, be prepared for the long haul. But, hold on. I don’t know about you, but on most days, I don’t have 40 minutes to spare, even for very interesting comparative analyses. Fortunately, to avoid 100s of iterations of the same operations on different data, we can make use of a very common work-around in scientific computing: parallelization, the simultaneous execution of different pieces of a larger computation across multiple computing processors or cores. In fact, this sort of problem is considered embarrassingly parallel, that is, it is so very easy to split this task into many parallel subtasks. Specifically, we have 500 identical tasks; we need only to break these up into subtasks that run in parallel. That’s what we’ll do.

R has several packages for parallel computing. Perhaps the most versatile in the future package. It works across operating systems and has a relatively simple workflows.

future_lapply() from the future.apply package is simply an extension of lapply() using the functions of future that takes the tasks performed on each item in a list and spreads the tasks across multiple cores on a computer. This is perfect for embarrassingly parallel problems.

For example, say we wanted to do something that’s simple, but takes just a little bit of time, like generating 10 million random values from a normal distribution (mean=0, sd=1) any number of times. Let’s encode a function that does this and allows one to specify how many times to do it.

my_fun <- function(x){for(i in x) rnorm(1e7)}

For comparison, let’s first use the non-parallel lapply() to run the function 50 times and then use a typical workflow using future_lapply() to do the same. We’ll breakdown what’s happening here afterward.

library(future)
library(future.apply)
# Run sequentially
set.seed(1234) #for repetition
t_nomc <- system.time(
  lapply(1:50, my_fun)
)

# Run in parallel
# Set up parallel R sessions, leaving two cores available

plan(
  multisession,
  workers = availableCores(omit = 2)
)

set.seed(1234) #for repetition
t_mc <- system.time(
  future_lapply(1:50, my_fun, future.seed = TRUE)
)

# Shut down the background workers
plan(sequential)

print(t_nomc)
##    user  system elapsed 
##  12.848   0.323  13.206
print(t_mc)
##    user  system elapsed 
##   1.056   0.157   7.030

plan() controls how the work runs. It creates separate background parallel R sessions and works on Windows, macOS, and Linux. Each session will be a worker, and the work of the 50 operations is distributed across workers rather than repeated in full by every worker. With workers = availableCores(omit = 2), we’re asking that we distribute the work over the number of available cores minus two usings one of future’s handy functions for determing the number of cores. Most modern laptops and desktops have at lease a half dozen cores, but we don’t want all of them to be used by the operation. We should leave a few cores that the computer can use for operations running in the background.

future_lapply() takes the identical arguments—list then function to apply over list values—but has a few more devoted to controlling the parallel operations. Because my_fun() generates random values, we use future.seed = TRUE. The other set.seed() calls also make for reproducible parallel results.

We should shut down the parallel session when we don’t need it and that requires that we run plan(sequential), returning operations back to sequential.

Note that we ran the sequential and parallel operations in system.time() so that we can record how long each takes. As you can see, the parallelized operation was considerably faster than lapply(). You may be asking, “Wait, if we divided the work over 6 cores, why didn’t we see a 6-fold increase in speed?” That’s a great question. We don’t see that linear increase because starting workers and transferring data takes time, that is, parallelization has overhead. A parallel apparach most useful when each task takes enough time to outweigh that overhead. Like say, say running PGLS models and evolutionary rates analyses on large data sets.

Thus, this framework looks like it could really speed up our estimation of body shape evolutionary rates.
